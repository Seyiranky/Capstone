# Keep Pindo, sandbox with Africa's Talking

Isoko Talents should keep **Pindo as its primary SMS provider** and add **Africa's Talking (AT) as the fallback driver and demo sandbox**. The Pindo driver already in `server/src/lib/channels/sms.ts` is a good fit. Pindo is a Kigali company with a one-call Bearer-token REST API, it advertises Kinyarwanda support, and it lists Irembo as a customer. Its published price starts at **$0.01 per SMS**, which puts a 200-user pilot at about **$20–40 a month**. Twilio and other global providers would charge **$420–650 a month** for the same traffic. The provider choice is not the main constraint, though. The sender ID is. On MTN and Airtel Rwanda, only a **pre-registered alphanumeric sender ID** delivers reliably. Registering one needs an RDB company certificate and stamped letterhead letters, and takes roughly **three weeks**. A student team without a registered entity cannot finish that on its own. The practical plan has four parts: keep the `log` driver as the default, add an `africastalking` driver so assessors can watch messages arrive in AT's free simulator, use Pindo test credits for one recorded live send to a team member's phone, and file the "Isoko" sender ID through a sponsoring registered organisation in parallel. Three things matter in code: normalising text to GSM-7 (which keeps Kinyarwanda at 160 characters per segment), offering opt-out through app settings rather than SMS replies, and stating plainly in the consent text which processor receives phone numbers.

## Local aggregators cost 25–40 times less than global APIs

Price separates the options more than any other factor. Pindo advertises SMS **"starting at $0.01 per SMS"**, with MTN Rwanda as its calculator example, and Verify at $0.002 per successful verification ([Pindo pricing](https://www.pindo.io/pricing/)). Africa's Talking charges in RWF on spend-based tiers: **RWF 12 per SMS to MTN and RWF 10 to Airtel** at the entry tier, falling to RWF 8 and RWF 6 at higher spend ([AT pricing](https://africastalking.com/pricing)). That figure comes from a search snippet, because the live page returned HTTP 403. Celcom Africa quotes about RWF 11 (€0.01) per SMS over direct MTN and Airtel connections ([Celcom Africa](https://celcomafrica.com/bulk-sms-rwanda)). The global providers sit an order of magnitude higher: Twilio charges **$0.3261 per segment** to Rwanda, plus a $0.025 pumping-protection fee ([Twilio Rwanda pricing](https://www.twilio.com/en-us/sms/pricing/rw)), and Plivo charges $0.2522 to MTN and Airtel ([Plivo Rwanda pricing](https://www.plivo.com/sms/pricing/rw/)).

The pilot scenario is 200 users receiving 10 messages each, or 2,000 messages a month. The table below works it through. Exchange rates are approximate (about RWF 1,400–1,450 to the dollar, not sourced).

| Provider | 2,000 × 1 segment (GSM-7) | 2,000 × 2 segments (Unicode or long) |
|---|---|---|
| Africa's Talking, MTN entry tier RWF 12 | RWF 24,000 (~$17) | RWF 48,000 (~$34) |
| Pindo, "from $0.01" | $20 | $40 |
| Celcom, €0.01 | ~$22 | ~$44 |
| Plivo, $0.2522 | ~$504 | ~$1,009 |
| Twilio, $0.3261 | ~$652 | ~$1,304 |

Pindo's "starting at" price is probably the MTN rate, and Airtel may cost more. Neither its Airtel rate nor how it bills Unicode segments is published, so a **1.5× budget buffer** is sensible. Verification shows the same gap. Twilio Verify costs $0.05 plus the Rwanda SMS leg, about **$0.38 per verification** ([Twilio Verify pricing](https://www.twilio.com/en-us/verify/pricing)). Verifying all 200 users once would cost about $76 there, against a few dollars for a home-built OTP sent through the existing driver. Firebase phone auth would add a second vendor and a paid Blaze billing account ([Logto](https://blog.logto.io/fr/firebase-authentication-pricing)) for little gain.

## Pindo wins on fit, while AT wins on tooling and resilience

Pindo is the local choice an assessor will expect. It was founded in Kigali in 2020, has raised over $833K from investors including Google and Mozilla Ventures ([VC4A](https://vc4a.com/ventures/pindo-rwanda-ltd/)), and lists **Irembo, Kudibooks and Exuus** as customers for payment notifications and reminders ([Pindo](https://pindo.io/)). It also builds Kinyarwanda speech tooling: it launched a Kinyarwanda voice AI banking agent with I&M Bank ([The New Times](https://www.newtimes.co.rw/article/37739/news/featured/pindo-im-bank-launch-rwandas-first-kinyarwanda-voice-ai-banking-agent/amp)). Its API is the simplest available. `POST https://api.pindo.io/v1/sms/` takes `{to, text, sender}` with a Bearer token, `/v1/sms/bulk` accepts a `recipients[]` list with names for personalisation, and there are verify and inbound endpoints ([pindo-cli README](https://github.com/pindoio/pindo-cli)). Isoko's driver already makes exactly that call.

Pindo has three weaknesses. First, there is **no Node SDK**. The `pindoio` GitHub organisation holds only a Python CLI, last updated June 2025 ([GitHub](https://github.com/pindoio)), which signals a small team. Second, its documentation has moved from docs.pindo.io, which no longer resolves, to [docs.pindo.ai](https://docs.pindo.ai). Third, the delivery-report webhook page returned 404, so the status values Isoko would record are unverified. None of these blocks a pilot. Together they argue for keeping a second driver ready behind `SMS_DRIVER`.

Africa's Talking is the right second driver. Its `africastalking` npm package is actively maintained: version 0.8.4 was published on 2026-10-09 and the package gets about 15,000 weekly downloads ([npm](https://www.npmjs.com/package/africastalking)). AT offers the only sandbox in this field that suits a demo. It is **free and never charged**, and it shows SMS, USSD, short codes and payments on a web or Android simulator instead of a real handset ([AT help](https://help.africastalking.com/en/articles/2189460-what-are-the-sandbox-and-the-live-environments); [AT simulator](https://developers.africastalking.com/simulator)). Sender IDs in Rwanda are currently free through AT ([AT community help](https://community.africastalking.com/forum/help-18238/topic/at-help-comprehensive-94/)). AT should not be the sole production dependency, however. It is in a **governance dispute**: a founder was ousted, a new CEO was sworn in in March 2026, and in July 2026 the High Court referred the disputes to ICC arbitration ([Kenya Insights](https://kenyainsights.com/africas-talking-ceo-cry-how-the-world-banks-ifc-sabotage-killed-my-company)). That outlet has an editorial angle, but Tracxn still lists about 95 staff and no shutdown ([Tracxn](https://tracxn.com/d/companies/africas-talking/__Vk3YEsKZ4b0w6fmDafAioJV2SQFXXB8n01Jo_x_aPs4)). AT's top-up documentation also mentions M-Pesa and bank transfer, not Rwandan MoMo ([AT help](https://help.africastalking.com/en/articles/2255714-how-do-i-top-up-my-mobile-data-wallet)).

Two other providers were considered and ruled out. IntouchSMS, a Kigali company, offers an HTTP API, SMPP and flat-rate pricing across networks, but publishes no prices and is oriented to purchase orders and commercial agreements ([IntouchSMS pricing](https://www.intouchsms.co.rw/pricing)). That is too much friction for a student team. Twilio has the best SDK and a free trial, but besides its price, **two-way SMS, short codes and domestic long codes are unsupported in Rwanda** ([Twilio Rwanda guidelines](https://www.twilio.com/en-us/guidelines/rw/sms)). It also recorded an MTN Rwanda delivery delay on 2024-10-09 ([Twilio status](https://status.twilio.com/incidents/sdl270ch6qtj)). The case for Twilio is a polished global vendor with clear compliance documents. That case falls apart on a budget measured in tens of dollars.

## The sender ID, not the provider, is the critical path

The comment in `sms.ts` is broadly right, but it merges two separate approvals. RURA runs a **free "Sender ID Whitelisting Authorization"** e-service. It requires a signed request letter, an agent authorisation letter if a third party files it, and an **RDB registration certificate**, and RURA states that it completes "within 5 days" ([RURA licensing portal](https://licensing.rura.rw/licenses/license-details/eyJpdiI6Iit3cEt1Y2Z2RzJNODhITUxuSTBMZEE9PSIsInZhbHVlIjoiWm52UEFMUEc0Z0tBSzhueit2RHdBUT09IiwibWFjIjoiMDM2MTUzM2ZhN2I5YmE5YzA1ODI5MGRkN2JkMjVjNTYxY2QxMjZiZTEyNDYxZDc0N2I0OGI2ZmE4ZmVlMmI2NyIsInRhZyI6IiJ9/license)). Separately, MTN and Airtel each require operator pre-registration, filed through the provider, with **signed and stamped procuration letters on company letterhead**. That step takes **about three weeks** ([Telerivet, Feb 2026](https://www.telerivet.com/blog/rwanda-sms-compliance-rura-sender-id-law-058); [Twilio Rwanda guidelines](https://www.twilio.com/en-us/guidelines/rw/sms)). Generic IDs such as "INFO", "ALERTS" and "VERIFY" are rejected. An ID may have at most 11 characters and must start with a letter, and dynamic, unregistered senders are not supported ([Sent.dm](https://sent.dm/en/resources/sms-compliance/rw-sms-guidance)). Twilio adds that **promotional content cannot be registered**, which favours Isoko's transactional use: gig matches, booking confirmations and payment receipts. "Isoko", the driver's default `SMS_SENDER_ID`, meets the format rules. D7 claims that Airtel accepts dynamic sender IDs ([D7](https://d7networks.com/sms/rwanda/sms-regulations)), but three other sources disagree, so plan to register on both networks.

Skipping registration does not work. Numeric international senders are **not supported on MTN or Airtel**: they fail on MTN and are altered on Airtel ([Twilio](https://www.twilio.com/en-us/guidelines/rw/sms)). A cheap provider that skips registration is probably using a grey route, which means random local senders and unpredictable blocking ([Enea](https://www.enea.com/insights/network-messaging-fraud-grey-routes-sim-banks/)). AT warns that accounts which keep using its shared `AFRICASTKNG` sender after getting their own ID can be put on **"risk hold"** ([AT help](https://help.africastalking.com/en/articles/2258523-why-am-i-getting-the-risk-hold-error)). The realistic route to a branded ID is to file through a **registered sponsoring entity**, such as the university or an incubator. That entity supplies the RDB certificate and letterhead, and filing should start at least four weeks before any live date.

Two-way SMS should stay out of scope. Telerivet states that A2P SMS in Rwanda is one-way and that no short codes are available ([Telerivet](https://www.telerivet.com/blog/rwanda-sms-compliance-rura-sender-id-law-058)). Pindo and Intouch advertise two-way messaging through local short codes ([Pindo](https://www.pindo.io); [IntouchSMS](https://www.intouchsms.co.rw/)). The most likely explanation is that local aggregators hold short codes while global ones cannot, but this is unconfirmed. RURA prices short codes at **USD 200–1,000** ([RURA portal](https://licensing.rura.rw/licenses/license-details/eyJpdiI6Iit3cEt1Y2Z2RzJNODhITUxuSTBMZEE9PSIsInZhbHVlIjoiWm52UEFMUEc0Z0tBSzhueit2RHdBUT09IiwibWFjIjoiMDM2MTUzM2ZhN2I5YmE5YzA1ODI5MGRkN2JkMjVjNTYxY2QxMjZiZTEyNDYxZDc0N2I0OGI2ZmE4ZmVlMmI2NyIsInRhZyI6IiJ9/license)). For feature-phone workers, the established Rwandan pattern is USSD: One Acre Fund enrolled over 600,000 farmers through it ([One Acre Fund](https://oneacrefund.org/articles/using-mobile-tech-shape-smallholder-farming-rwanda)). A shared USSD code through an aggregator costs about RWF 26,000 a month ([HelloDuty](https://helloduty.com/country/rwanda)). That should be presented as a costed next step and prototyped in AT's sandbox, not built for production now. Because replies are impossible on one-way routes, **"reply STOP" cannot work**. The per-user channel preference Isoko already has is the correct opt-out mechanism.

## Law 058/2021 makes consent text and processor choice deliverables

Rwanda's data protection law treats Isoko as a controller and its SMS gateway as a processor. Both must in principle **register with NCSA's Data Protection and Privacy Office** (Article 29), which issues a certificate within 30 days ([DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/index.html?c=RW&t=law)). Registration is done through dpo.gov.rw ([Digital Policy Alert](https://digitalpolicyalert.org/change/12081-dpo-registration-guide-for-data-controllers-and-data-processors)). Article 50 requires that personal data, phone numbers included, **be stored in Rwanda** unless NCSA authorises otherwise. A transfer abroad needs an Article 48 basis, such as consent or NCSA-approved safeguards, and Article 49 requires a written contract with the recipient. Consent must be **freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous**, with no pre-ticked boxes, and can be withdrawn at any time ([Telerivet](https://www.telerivet.com/blog/rwanda-sms-compliance-rura-sender-id-law-058)). Breaches must be reported to NCSA within 48 hours. Sent.dm's claim that breaches go to RURA is wrong. Fines run from RWF 2–5 million or 1% of turnover ([DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/index.html?c=RW&t=law)).

This favours Pindo for a second reason. A Rwandan processor removes the cross-border step from the SMS leg. It does not remove the step if Isoko's own Postgres database is hosted abroad. For the capstone, the defensible position has four parts. The SMS opt-in should be unticked by default and should name the provider. The privacy notice should state where data is processed. Opt-out should work in-app at any time. NCSA registration should be listed as a pre-launch task. Unsolicited commercial messages must identify the sender and offer an opt-out under RURA's 2018 regulation and Article 168 of the ICT law, and spamming is a criminal offence ([Digital Policy Alert](https://digitalpolicyalert.org/change/12964-regulation-on-user-rights-for-unsolicited-commercial-communications-regulation-no-001rstdcmrrura018); [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/index.html?c=RW&t=law)). Any "new gigs this week" digest should therefore start with "Isoko:" and include a settings link. Rwanda has no statutory quiet hours, but providers recommend sending non-urgent messages between **08:00 and 20:00 CAT** ([Telerivet](https://www.telerivet.com/blog/rwanda-sms-compliance-rura-sender-id-law-058)). Scheduling digests in that window is easy and worth doing.

## A curly apostrophe can triple the cost of a Kinyarwanda message

Standard Kinyarwanda uses **23 Latin letters without diacritics** and leaves tone unmarked ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinyarwanda); [UT Austin](https://www.laits.utexas.edu/phonology/kinyarwanda/kin_tone.html)). Well-formed Kinyarwanda therefore fits **GSM-7: 160 characters for a single message, 153 per segment when concatenated**. UCS-2 allows only 70 and 67 ([Sent.dm](https://sent.dm/en/resources/sms-compliance/rw-sms-guidance)). The risk is the frequent elisions such as *n'* and *y'*. Translators, word processors and phone keyboards often turn the apostrophe into a curly ’. A single curly apostrophe switches the whole message to Unicode. A 150-character message then grows from one segment to three, and a 200-character message from two segments to three. The current driver passes `text` through unchanged. Before Kinyarwanda templates go live, it should map ’ ‘ “ ” to their ASCII forms, strip emoji, and log a segment count. The segment count also makes the cost table above something Isoko can check against its own data. Kinyarwanda words also tend to be longer than their English equivalents, so templates should be drafted to fit 160 characters in both languages.

The demo path for assessors follows from these constraints. The first layer is the per-user preference screen and the `[sms:log]` output, which shows that the abstraction works end to end. The second layer is `SMS_DRIVER=africastalking` pointed at AT's sandbox, where the same notification appears on the browser simulator at no cost and without a sender ID. The third layer is one real send through Pindo's free-signup test credits ([Pindo docs](https://docs.pindo.ai/)) to a team member's MTN number, **recorded in advance** in case live delivery fails during the assessment. Two points about that third layer are unconfirmed: how large Pindo's trial credit is, and whether trial accounts send from a shared default sender. Ask Pindo support in writing now, because the answer decides whether layer three is possible before a sponsored sender ID exists. A Twilio trial is a weaker substitute. It only reaches verified numbers, it only works in the sign-up country, and it allows only Twilio templates, not Isoko's own message text ([Twilio trial guide](https://www.twilio.com/docs/messaging/guides/how-to-use-your-free-trial-account)).

## Conclusion

The research shifts the main risk from vendor selection to paperwork and character encoding. Any of the cheap local aggregators would send Isoko's messages for well under the cost of a phone bundle. None of them can deliver a branded message without a registered organisation's stamp, and all of them charge triple for a mistyped apostrophe. The `SMS_DRIVER` design already in the codebase is therefore the strongest part of the SMS story. The capstone should present a working default (`log`), a free visible demo (AT sandbox), a primary production provider (Pindo), and a documented compliance path, rather than claim live delivery it cannot guarantee.

Three open questions should be closed with direct emails rather than more desk research: whether Pindo's trial includes a shared sender, what Pindo charges for Airtel and for Unicode segments, and what its delivery-report status values are. A further point follows from the evidence. Isoko's actual users are workers on feature phones, and the Rwandan services that reach those users run on USSD. The more ambitious next phase is a shared USSD code, prototyped free in AT's sandbox, not two-way SMS.
